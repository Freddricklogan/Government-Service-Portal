# UX Research Findings: Digital Service Portal

## Research Overview

This document presents the findings from a comprehensive user experience research study conducted to inform the design of the Digital Service Portal. The research employed a mixed-methods approach combining qualitative interviews, contextual inquiry, usability testing, and quantitative surveys to understand how citizens currently interact with government services and what they need from a digital portal.

### Research Timeline
- Discovery research: 8 weeks
- Usability testing: 4 weeks (3 rounds)
- Survey deployment: 2 weeks with rolling analysis

### Participants
- 42 citizen interviews across 6 demographic segments
- 18 contextual inquiry sessions at government offices
- 24 usability testing participants across 3 rounds
- 1,200 survey respondents from the general public

## Personas

### Persona 1: Maria Santos -- The Working Parent

**Demographics:** Age 34, married, two children (ages 5 and 8), works full-time as a medical assistant, household income $52,000

**Digital Literacy:** Moderate. Uses smartphone daily for social media, messaging, and basic tasks. Comfortable with familiar apps but anxious about unfamiliar digital processes, especially those involving official documents.

**Government Service Needs:**
- Enrolling children in after-school programs
- Renewing vehicle registration
- Accessing school meal assistance benefits
- Filing property tax payments

**Goals:**
- Complete government tasks quickly during breaks or after children are in bed
- Avoid taking time off work for in-person office visits
- Understand what documents are needed before starting a process
- Feel confident that online submissions are secure and properly received

**Pain Points:**
- Government websites use jargon she does not understand
- Cannot tell whether a process can be completed fully online or will require an office visit
- Afraid of making mistakes on forms that she cannot easily correct
- Has had experiences where online submissions seemed to "disappear" with no confirmation
- Mobile experience on government sites is often broken or unusable

**Quote:** "I just need to know: can I do this on my phone during lunch, or do I need to take a day off work? Just tell me that up front."

### Persona 2: James Washington -- The Senior Citizen

**Demographics:** Age 71, retired military, lives alone, fixed income of $38,000 from pension and Social Security

**Digital Literacy:** Low to moderate. Has a tablet his daughter set up for him. Can use email and visit bookmarked websites but struggles with complex navigation, small text, and multi-step online processes.

**Government Service Needs:**
- Managing veteran benefits
- Property tax senior exemptions
- Medicare-related services
- Requesting replacement documents (DD-214, birth certificate)

**Goals:**
- Maintain independence by handling his own affairs
- Avoid asking family members for help with "simple" tasks
- Not feel embarrassed by technology he does not understand
- Have a phone number to call when he gets stuck

**Pain Points:**
- Text is too small on most government websites
- Timeout sessions log him out while he is still reading and understanding the page
- Password requirements are frustrating and he forgets them
- Captcha systems are very difficult for him to complete
- Does not trust that information submitted online is secure
- When he encounters an error, there is no clear way to get help

**Quote:** "I served this country for 28 years. I should not need a computer science degree to renew my property tax exemption."

### Persona 3: Aisha Johnson -- The Small Business Owner

**Demographics:** Age 28, single, owns a small catering business, household income $44,000

**Digital Literacy:** High. Digital native comfortable with technology. Uses multiple apps and platforms for business management.

**Government Service Needs:**
- Business license renewal
- Health department permits
- Tax filings and payments
- Hiring-related compliance (workers' comp, tax withholding)
- Event permits for off-site catering

**Goals:**
- Complete government requirements as efficiently as possible so she can focus on her business
- Understand all requirements up front to avoid surprises
- Track the status of applications and permits in real time
- Receive proactive reminders about upcoming deadlines

**Pain Points:**
- Different permits require interactions with different agencies with no coordination
- Status of applications is opaque -- no tracking, no timeline estimates
- Requirements are scattered across multiple websites with inconsistent information
- Some processes require faxing or mailing physical documents despite being "online"
- Phone wait times for questions are unacceptable (reports 45+ minutes average)

**Quote:** "I need permits from three different departments and none of them talk to each other. Each one has a different website, different forms, different requirements. I spend more time navigating government than running my business."

### Persona 4: David Park -- The New Resident

**Demographics:** Age 40, recently relocated from another state, IT professional, household income $95,000

**Digital Literacy:** Very high. Software professional who has strong expectations for digital experiences based on private sector standards.

**Government Service Needs:**
- New state driver's license
- Vehicle registration transfer
- Voter registration
- Child school enrollment
- Utility connections
- Property tax registration

**Goals:**
- Complete all "new resident" tasks in the minimum number of interactions
- Understand the sequence: what must be done first, second, third
- Be told exactly what documents are needed so he can gather them once
- Complete as much as possible online before any required office visits

**Pain Points:**
- No "new resident" pathway that bundles related services
- Each agency requires re-entering the same basic information
- Conflicting information about what identification documents are accepted
- Some services require in-person verification but the appointment systems are booked weeks out
- No single place to understand all the things a new resident must do

**Quote:** "In the private sector, we would never make a customer enter the same information six times. Why does government?"

## Journey Maps

### Journey: License Renewal (Current State)

**Stage 1: Awareness (1-2 weeks before expiration)**
- Citizen receives mailed reminder (if address is current) or notices expiration date on license
- Emotions: mild concern, uncertainty about process
- Pain point: 22% of survey respondents said they did not receive a reminder

**Stage 2: Research (30-60 minutes)**
- Citizen searches online for renewal information
- Visits government website, navigates through multiple pages
- Tries to determine if they are eligible for online renewal
- Emotions: confusion, frustration with website navigation
- Pain point: Eligibility criteria are buried 4 clicks deep; 38% of participants could not find them

**Stage 3: Preparation (1-3 days)**
- Citizen gathers required documents (proof of address, current license, payment method)
- Some discover they need a vision test or updated photo, requiring an office visit
- Emotions: frustration if additional requirements are discovered late
- Pain point: 41% of research participants were surprised by a requirement they did not expect

**Stage 4: Submission (15-45 minutes)**
- Online: Fill out form, upload documents, pay fee
- In-person: Travel to office, wait in line (average 47 minutes observed), complete paperwork
- Emotions: anxiety about doing it correctly (online), frustration with wait (in-person)
- Pain point: Online form does not save progress; 28% of participants lost work due to timeout

**Stage 5: Confirmation and Follow-Up (1-4 weeks)**
- Receive confirmation email or receipt
- Wait for new license to arrive by mail
- Emotions: uncertainty about whether application was processed correctly
- Pain point: No tracking capability; 56% of respondents said they worried about whether the renewal went through

## Key Research Findings

### Finding 1: Trust is the Foundation
67% of survey respondents rated "trust and security" as their top concern when using government services online. Citizens need visible indicators of legitimacy and security. The government brand itself carries authority, but poor design undermines that trust.

### Finding 2: Mobile is Not Optional
54% of participants attempted government service tasks on mobile devices. Of those, 73% reported a poor experience. Government digital services must be mobile-first, not mobile-adapted.

### Finding 3: Plain Language is Essential
In usability testing, participants were asked to interpret 10 common government phrases. Average comprehension was only 42%. Terms like "adjudication," "remittance," and "affidavit" confused the majority of participants. When the same concepts were explained in plain language, comprehension rose to 91%.

### Finding 4: Status Visibility Reduces Anxiety
The single most requested feature across all personas and research methods was the ability to track application status. 78% of survey respondents said they would use a status tracking feature. Currently, 64% of citizens call government offices primarily to check on application status -- calls that could be eliminated with self-service tracking.

### Finding 5: Error Prevention Over Error Correction
In usability testing, the most frustrating experiences occurred when citizens completed a lengthy form only to receive a vague error message. Inline validation, progress saving, clear requirements stated up front, and the ability to correct mistakes without restarting were the most valued design features.

### Finding 6: Bundled Services Save Time
When asked to complete the tasks associated with a life event (e.g., moving to a new address), participants averaged 4.2 separate website visits and spent 2.7 hours total. When presented with a bundled service concept that combined related tasks, estimated time dropped to 45 minutes and satisfaction increased dramatically.

### Finding 7: Accessibility Gaps are Widespread
In accessibility testing with participants using assistive technology, 83% of current government web pages had at least one WCAG 2.1 AA violation that blocked task completion. The most common issues were: missing form labels, inadequate color contrast, inaccessible PDFs, and missing alt text on informational images.

### Finding 8: Digital Does Not Mean Exclusive
Even among digitally comfortable users, 71% wanted the option to call a human for complex or sensitive transactions. The ideal model is "digital first, not digital only" -- optimize for online completion while maintaining phone, mail, and in-person channels.

## Design Recommendations

Based on the research findings, the following design recommendations guided the portal prototype:

1. **Clear government identity**: Prominent official branding that immediately establishes legitimacy
2. **Search-first discovery**: Prominent search bar on the homepage, since 61% of participants preferred searching to browsing
3. **Service cards with descriptions**: Clear, plain-language descriptions of what each service category includes
4. **Action-oriented labels**: "Access Service" rather than "Learn More" or "Click Here"
5. **Visible accessibility commitment**: Accessibility statement in the footer with a direct contact for accessibility issues
6. **Phone and in-person fallback**: Visible phone number and office hours for citizens who need or prefer human assistance
7. **Responsive design**: Full functionality on all screen sizes, with touch-friendly interaction targets
8. **ARIA labels throughout**: Comprehensive accessibility markup for screen reader compatibility
9. **Skip navigation link**: Allows keyboard users to bypass repetitive navigation
10. **High contrast and readable typography**: Minimum 4.5:1 contrast ratio and 16px base font size
